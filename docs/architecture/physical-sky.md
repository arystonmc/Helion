# Helion Architecture: Physical Sky

Part of the [Helion architecture](../ARCHITECTURE.md).

## Physical Sky

Off by default (`physicalSky.enabled`). In the Overworld skybox without a custom sky renderer, with the camera in air (not in water, lava or powder snow), the flat vanilla sky color, the vanilla sun, the sunrise glow and the vanilla fog color are replaced by a sky computed from how sunlight and moonlight scatter in an Earth-like atmosphere, with its own sun disc and twilight glow. Its own sun, a three-dimensional moon and a procedural night sky replace the vanilla sun, moon and stars. Everywhere else `SkyStage` draws the vanilla sky.

| Pass | Writes | Shader |
|---|---|---|
| `sky_transmittance` | persistent 256 x 64, four RGBA16_FLOAT targets, written once | `transmittance.fsh`: spectral transmittance to the top of the atmosphere per height and view angle |
| `sky_multiple_scattering` | persistent 32 x 32, four RGBA16_FLOAT targets, written once | `multiple_scattering.fsh`: spectral second order scattering over 64 directions with ground albedo, extended to infinite orders with the geometric series |
| `sky_view_sun`, `sky_view_moon` | persistent 192 x 108, two RGBA16_FLOAT targets each, redrawn only when the light height or the camera altitude changed | `sky_view.fsh`: spectral scattering per view direction relative to the light, finer near the horizon and the light, converted to linear sRGB; target 0 holds Rayleigh single scattering with its phase plus multiple scattering, target 1 Mie single scattering without its phase |
| `sky_horizon` | frame 1 x 1 RGBA8 | `horizon.fsh`: average sky color of 32 horizontal directions, read back to the CPU for the cloud fog |
| `sky_light` | frame 3 x 1 RGBA16_FLOAT | `sky_light.fsh`: sun light and moon light at the camera height after the atmosphere, and the irradiance of the sky dome on an upward surface, for the clouds and the light shafts |
| `sky_celestials` | nothing visible | `renderCelestials` at the vanilla sky place: only the vanilla dark disc and the after sky event |
| `sky`, after `opaque_geometry` | scene color where the depth is still the far plane | `sky.fsh`, drawn with a depth test against the far plane, so only sky pixels run it (1.1 to 1.2 ms of stars, Milky Way and aurora at night in 1080p dropped to near zero indoors): both tables of a light, the Mie part times the Mie phase of the exact angle between the pixel and the light; sun sky times the sun brightness plus moon sky times the moon brightness, plus the twilight glow, the sun disc and the moon, rain dims and greys it, the sky grade, Neutral encoding |
| fog, drawn inside `transparent_geometry` | scene color, blended | `fog.fsh`: vanilla fog distances, physical sky color in the direction of each pixel |

- **Method.** Follows Hillaire, "A Scalable and Production Ready Sky and Atmosphere Rendering Technique" (EGSR 2020), with the transmittance parameterization of Bruneton and Neyret (2008) and Earth parameters (Rayleigh, Mie with g = 0.8, ozone); own implementation from the publications. 1 block is 1 m; the camera height above sea level moves the viewer up in the atmosphere. Sun and moon directions come from the vanilla sun and moon angles exactly as vanilla rotates the celestial bodies, so the vanilla sun sits in the middle of the scattering glow.
- **Spectral color.** Light is computed at 16 wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm (20 nm apart), packed as four RGBA groups, so the lookup tables are written with four color targets in one pass. The sky view passes turn the spectrum into linear sRGB with weights from the CIE 1931 color matching functions and the XYZ to sRGB matrix, white balanced to the solar spectrum, so direct sunlight is white and sunsets get their orange from the atmosphere alone. Rayleigh and ozone coefficients per wavelength, the solar spectrum and the CIE tables are in `AtmosphereSpectrum` (data from Bruneton's Precomputed Atmospheric Scattering, BSD-3, see `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md`); `AtmosphereSpectrumTest` checks them against Hillaire's RGB coefficients within 1 %. The per-wavelength values reach the shaders in the constant `HelionSpectrum` buffer.
- **Brightness.** `PhysicalSkyResources.SUN_ILLUMINANCE` (16) puts the noon zenith at the vanilla brightness after Neutral encoding; `MOON_ILLUMINANCE` (0.08) gives a dark blue moonlit night close to vanilla's darkness. The sky is HDR around the sun, so bloom picks the glow up. Directions below the horizon use the horizon color, dimmed to 45 percent over the first 7 degrees below it, so the sky seen past the edge of the loaded terrain from high up and the fog toward the ground read as dark distant haze instead of a bright band.
- **Color grade.** The displayed sky and fog get a gentle saturation of 1.12 at day that rises to 1.3 in the golden hour (`helionGoldenHour`: the sun within 0.3 of the horizon, about 17 degrees), and skies dimmer than a luminance of 0.015 shift toward a cool blue (`HELION_SCOTOPIC_TINT`, up to 70 percent below 0.002), the way eyes see a moonlit sky. The aerosol (Mie) density is 0.75 of Hillaire's Earth value. The grade only changes what is displayed; the sky light that lights terrain uses the radiance with the twilight glow but without the grade. The vanilla sunrise fan is no longer drawn.
- **Sun disc.** `sky.fsh` draws the sun itself (`helionSunDisc`): 0.45 degrees of angular radius (vanilla's quad was about 24 degrees wide), limb darkened per channel and colored by the analytic transmittance of its air mass (Kasten and Young, Hillaire's RGB Rayleigh, Mie and ozone optical depths), so it turns orange and red as it sets; its edge is antialiased with `fwidth`. A tight glare (0.6 of the disc radiance, falling off over 0.3 degrees) and a wider aureole surround it, and once the light is bright enough in every channel it is bleached to white, so at day the disc dissolves into one white glare without a visible edge, the way a real sun is too bright to show its rim; low suns stay orange and show their disc. Only the sky pass adds it; fog, horizon and sky light do not.
- **Twilight.** The physical model alone keeps almost no orange once the sun is below the horizon, so `helionTwilightGlow` adds three bands around sunrise and sunset, strongest with the sun 0.6 degrees below the horizon and fading over about 10 degrees above and 3 degrees below: an ember to gold glow along the horizon toward the sun, a rose afterglow higher up once the sun has set, and the pink belt of Venus opposite the sun. It is part of the sky radiance, so fog, cloud fog and the sky dome light on terrain take the same tones.
- **Moon.** `helionMoonDisc` draws the moon as a lit sphere of 1.5 degrees of angular radius, larger than the sun on purpose, the way the moon looks to players. The vanilla moon phase reaches the shader as `MoonPhaseAngle` (phase index times 45 degrees, `SkyEnvironment.moonPhaseAngle`) and turns the light around the sphere, so the terminator is a real shadow line. The surface has Lommel-Seeliger reflectance (the full moon looks flat and bright like the real one), dark maria from low frequency noise, two sizes of craters with bowls and bright rims that also bend the normal so they cast relief along the terminator, faint earthshine on the dark side, and the same air mass reddening as the sun. The shading mixes Lommel-Seeliger with 45 % Lambert and the light sits 0.2 radians off the phase angle and slightly tilted, so even the full moon shows a rounded limb and crater relief instead of a flat disc. A soft blue-white halo (0.08 at the rim, falling off over 2 degrees, scaled by the lit fraction) shows that it gives light. Its frame follows the moon along its path, so the same face always points down at the world.
- **Night sky.** `helion_night.glsl` replaces the vanilla stars (`SkyRendererMixin` cancels `renderStars` under the physical sky). Stars are round points drawn with a Gaussian of 0.75 pixels (the pixel angle comes from the screen derivatives of the view ray), in two layers on a cube map grid of the star frame (14 and 46 cells per face side, at most one star per cell, the share of cells set by `StarDensity` and raised inside the Milky Way). The star frame turns with the vanilla star angle, so the sky rotates like vanilla. Brightness follows a steep power law, colors are blue, white, yellow and orange, and every star twinkles with two sines, more near the horizon. A star shows only when its peak is 1.5 to 5 times the sky behind it, so stars appear one by one at dusk, the faint ones vanish under a bright moon (the moon light scales with its lit fraction, down to none at new moon) and all of them hide in rain. A faint Milky Way band with a brighter core and dust lanes shows on dark skies (setting `milkyWay`). Three planets do not twinkle: Venus follows 0.75 radians behind the sun on its path, so it shines in the twilight, Jupiter and Mars sit fixed in the star frame. A meteor may cross the sky in any 7 second slot (18 percent), and on rare nights in snowy biomes (`AuroraNights`) aurora curtains of ridged noise glow green below and violet above. A navy night glow, brighter toward the horizon, keeps the night sky dark blue instead of black.
- **Fog.** Vanilla mixes the sunrise color into its fog color when the camera looks toward the sun, so a physical sky blended with vanilla fog changed color as the head turned. Under the physical sky `useFog` replaces the frame's `Fog` buffer (shared through `SceneFog`) with one that has no distance fog, so terrain, entities and translucents are drawn unfogged. The fog draw then fogs everything with depth with the vanilla environmental and render distance fog, but in the physical sky color of the pixel's direction, so fog and sky always meet without a seam. In the sorted path it runs after translucent terrain, entities and particles and before clouds, weather and the world border; in the order independent path after the OIT composite. Sky pixels (depth 0) are left alone.
- **Cloud fog.** Clouds fade with the vanilla cloud fog distance toward the color in the `Fog` buffer. That color is the average horizon color from `sky_horizon`, read back without stalling (`HorizonColorReadback`, three buffers, a few frames old); until the first readback arrives it is the vanilla fog color.
- **Glow around the sun and moon.** Mie scattering with g = 0.8 peaks about fifty times above its average within a few degrees of the light. Baked into the 192 x 108 table, the bilinear lookup moved that peak to the nearest table row (about 0.75° apart near the horizon), and the moon glow sat visibly beside the vanilla moon. The table therefore stores Mie scattering without its phase, and `helion_sky_color.glsl` multiplies it with the phase of the exact per-pixel angle, so the glow is always centered on the vanilla sun and moon.
- **View direction.** The sky and the fog take the direction of a pixel from two points on its ray (device depth 1.0 and 0.01) unprojected with the inverse level view projection. The level projection contains the view bobbing of `GameRenderer.bobView`, which also moves the eye by up to a tenth of a block; a single point unprojected close to the near plane would turn that shift into a large change of direction, and the sky would sway against the world while walking.
- **Sky light.** `sky_light` is published as `SkyLight` for the volumetric clouds, the light shafts and the underwater pass.
- **Cost.** The two constant tables are written once into persistent targets. The sky view tables depend only on the height of their light above the horizon and on the camera altitude, so they are persistent too and `SkyViewCache` redraws a table only when the height of its light changed by more than 2.5 x 10^-4 (about one tick of sun travel) or the camera moved more than one block up or down since it was drawn; before that they cost 0.37 and 0.28 ms every frame. Each frame costs about 0.8 to 1.6 ms on the development GPU (RTX 4050) when the tables are drawn: `sky_view_sun` and `sky_view_moon` 0.2 to 0.4 ms each, `sky_light` 0.2 to 1.1 ms. `sky_light` integrates the sky dome over 128 directions in only three fragments, so its time is mostly latency and varies a lot; spreading that work over more fragments is an open optimization.
- Aerial perspective (scattering in the air between the camera and terrain) is not part of it yet; the fog pass stands in for it at the vanilla distances.
